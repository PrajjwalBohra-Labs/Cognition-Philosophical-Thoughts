# Scientific Method

The scientific method provides a modern framework for disciplined empirical inquiry.

The classical philosophical materials in this repository are valuable for reasoning about evidence, inference, error, testimony, and justification, but they should not be treated as substitutes for modern experimental science.

---

# 1. Scientific Inquiry

A simplified workflow is:

```text
Observation
 ↓
Question
 ↓
Hypothesis
 ↓
Prediction
 ↓
Test
 ↓
Evidence
 ↓
Analysis
 ↓
Revision
```

Real scientific practice is more iterative and complex.

---

# 2. Observation and Perception

Scientific observation depends upon instruments, procedures, calibration, and interpretation.

This creates a useful epistemological question:

> When does observation provide sufficient grounds for knowledge?

Classical Nyāya can sharpen the conceptual distinction between perception, inference, memory, and testimony.

---

# 3. Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a proposed explanation or prediction.

Good practice requires:

- clear formulation;
- testable consequences;
- explicit assumptions;
- potential falsification;
- comparison with alternatives.

---

# 4. Inference

Scientific reasoning frequently moves from evidence to explanation.

Use:

```text
Evidence
 ↓
General relation / model
 ↓
Inference
 ↓
Hypothesis or conclusion
```

But scientific inference often involves probability, statistics, models, and uncertainty.

Do not reduce all scientific inference to classical *anumāna*.

---

# 5. Control and Counterexample

A strong scientific claim should survive attempts to defeat it.

Ask:

- What observation would count against it?
- What alternative explanation exists?
- What control is needed?
- Could the same result arise for another reason?

This is structurally compatible with the classical habit of testing proposed relations against counterexamples.

---

# 6. Reproducibility

A result becomes more trustworthy when:

- methods are explicit;
- data are available where appropriate;
- analysis can be inspected;
- independent researchers can reproduce or challenge the result.

This is a modern institutional development and should not be projected backward into classical philosophy.

---

# 7. Measurement

Separate:

```text
Observation
Measurement
Interpretation
Inference
Conclusion
```

Example:

```text
Instrument reading
 ↓
Measured quantity
 ↓
Interpretation
 ↓
Model
 ↓
Conclusion
```

Errors can enter at every stage.

---

# 8. Scientific Error

Distinguish:

- measurement error;
- sampling error;
- model error;
- procedural error;
- interpretation error;
- statistical error.

This can be compared conceptually with philosophical analyses of erroneous cognition.

The scientific mechanisms, however, must be investigated empirically.

---

# 9. Evidence Hierarchy

Do not use a single universal hierarchy.

Instead ask:

> What kind of evidence is appropriate for this question?

Examples:

```text
Historical question → documents / material evidence
Physical question → measurement / experiment
Mathematical question → proof
Philosophical question → argument / conceptual analysis
```

---

# 10. Practical Experiment Log

Use:

```text
Question:

Hypothesis:

Variables:

Assumptions:

Method:

Controls:

Measurements:

Results:

Unexpected observations:

Alternative explanations:

Limitations:

Conclusion:

What would change my mind?
```

---

# 11. Classical Comparison

For any scientific claim:

```text
What is observed?
What is inferred?
What is testified?
What assumptions are required?
What could defeat the inference?
```

This is especially useful when reading scientific journalism.

---

# 12. Correlation and Causation

Never infer causation simply from correlation.

Ask:

- What mechanism is proposed?
- What confounders exist?
- What controls are available?
- What alternative explanation fits the data?
- What intervention would test the causal claim?

---

# 13. Scientific Communication

When communicating results, distinguish:

```text
Established result
Strong interpretation
Tentative hypothesis
Speculation
Unknown
```

This is essential for intellectual honesty.

---

# 14. Practical Project

Choose a published scientific claim.

Create:

1. original claim;
2. source;
3. evidence;
4. method;
5. inference;
6. alternative explanation;
7. limitations;
8. confidence;
9. what further experiment would test it.
