# Philosophy of Mind

Philosophy of mind asks what minds are, what consciousness is, how cognition relates to the body, and what makes a person the same person over time.

The Indian philosophical materials in this repository offer especially important resources for thinking about self, mind, cognition, memory, agency, and liberation.

---

# 1. Core Questions

- What is the self?
- What is consciousness?
- What is cognition?
- Is mind identical with the self?
- Is mind identical with the body?
- How does memory relate to identity?
- What is agency?
- What is personal continuity?
- Can cognition exist independently of the body?
- What is liberation?

---

# 2. Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika Framework

Important terms include:

- *Ātman* — self;
- *Manas* — mind/internal organ;
- *Jñāna* — cognition;
- *Smṛti* — memory;
- *Icchā* — desire;
- *Dveṣa* — aversion;
- *Prayatna* — effort;
- *Sukha* — pleasure;
- *Duḥkha* — pain.

The system distinguishes the self from the body and from *manas*.

---

# 3. Self as a Philosophical Problem

Ask:

```text
What remains constant across changing experiences?
```

Possible evidence discussed in Nyāya includes:

- memory;
- recognition;
- desire;
- agency;
- cognition;
- ownership of experience.

The important task is to reconstruct the argument rather than merely assert that a self exists.

---

# 4. Manas

In Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika, *manas* is not simply equivalent to the brain.

It is an internal organ involved in cognition.

A simplified model is:

```text
Ātman
  +
Manas
  +
Sense / object conditions
      ↓
Cognition
```

The precise philosophical account should be studied from the primary texts.

---

# 5. Memory and Personal Identity

A central philosophical question is:

> What explains the experience that “I previously experienced that”?

Analyze:

```text
Past cognition
 ↓
Retention / disposition
 ↓
Present recollection
 ↓
Recognition of continuity
```

Then compare with modern theories of personal identity and memory.

---

# 6. Consciousness

Do not assume that all Indian traditions define consciousness in the same way.

Likewise, do not equate:

```text
Ātman = consciousness
```

without specifying the school.

For Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika, cognition is analyzed as a quality associated with the self rather than simply identifying the self with cognition.

This distinction is philosophically important.

---

# 7. Body and Self

Nyāya develops arguments against reducing the self to the body.

Useful analytical questions:

- Does bodily change eliminate personal identity?
- Can memory be explained purely by bodily continuity?
- What explains ownership of different cognitions?
- What is the subject of desire and agency?

---

# 8. Mind and Modern Neuroscience

Modern neuroscience investigates:

- neural correlates;
- attention;
- memory;
- perception;
- decision-making;
- brain injury;
- consciousness.

These provide empirical evidence about biological mechanisms.

They do not automatically settle metaphysical questions about the self.

---

# 9. Contemporary Positions

Compare classical positions with modern theories such as:

- physicalism;
- functionalism;
- dualism;
- identity theory;
- emergentism;
- embodied cognition;
- higher-order theories;
- global workspace approaches.

The purpose is comparative analysis, not forced equivalence.

# 11. Thought Experiments

Use carefully:

### Memory loss

If a person loses all autobiographical memories, is the same self still present?

### Brain replacement

If the biological brain is progressively replaced, what preserves personal identity?

### Perfect simulation

If a system reproduces every behavioral response, does it have cognition?

### Split agency

If different processes within one organism produce conflicting decisions, what is the agent?

These are philosophical questions, not empirical demonstrations.

---

# 12. AI and Mind

Ask:

- Does linguistic competence imply cognition?
- Does memory imply a self?
- Does successful inference imply understanding?
- Can a system have testimony without a speaker?
- What would count as evidence for machine consciousness?

Use the classical distinctions as analytical tools without claiming that classical authors addressed modern machines.
