# Language

Language is one of the major meeting points of Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā, and Vyākaraṇa.

The central questions include:

- How do words convey meaning?
- How does a sentence produce cognition?
- What is the relation between word and object?
- What makes verbal testimony authoritative?
- Is sentence meaning reducible to individual words?
- What is *śabda*?
- What is *sphoṭa*?

# 2. Nyāya: Language as a Source of Knowledge

Nyāya treats testimony as a means of valid cognition.

A basic structure is:

```text
Reliable speaker
      ↓
Verbal statement
      ↓
Linguistic understanding
      ↓
Cognition
```

The Nyāya texts define the reliable speaker in terms of knowledge of the relevant object and appropriate communication.

---

# 3. Word and Meaning

Nyāya examines competing theories of what words denote.

Possible candidates discussed in the broader tradition include:

- individual;
- universal;
- configuration;
- combinations of these.

The philosophical problem is:

> What exactly is cognized when a word is understood?

Do not assume that ordinary reference theory provides the answer.

---

# 4. Sentence Meaning

A sentence raises a more complex problem than an isolated word.

The analysis must account for:

- words;
- their meanings;
- their syntactic relations;
- expectancy;
- dependence;
- contextual unity;
- resulting cognition.

---

# 5. Sphoṭa Debate

The Nyāya material in the repository explicitly discusses and criticizes the *sphoṭa* theory associated with grammarians.

The issue can be represented as:

```text
Letters / sounds
      ↓
Do they themselves convey the unified meaning?
      ↓
If not:
      ↓
Is another linguistic entity required?
      ↓
Sphoṭa
```

The Nyāya response argues that conventional letters can account for verbal cognition without positing a separate *sphoṭa* entity.

This is a genuine philosophical disagreement, not merely a terminological difference.

# 6. Bhartṛhari

The *Vākyapadīya* approaches language from a different philosophical perspective.

The repository includes:

Important themes include:

- Śabda

- Meaning
- Sphoṭa

Bhartṛhari should be studied on his own terms before comparing his position with Nyāya.

---

# 7. Mīmāṃsā

Mīmāṃsā gives language a central role in the determination of *dharma*.

Important questions include:

- How is Vedic authority established?
- How are sentences understood?
- What is the status of words?
- How does verbal knowledge operate?
- What makes Vedic statements authoritative?

The Vaiśeṣika material in the repository records a direct disagreement with Mīmāṃsā concerning the eternality of word and the authority of the Veda.

---

# 8. Three Frameworks

A useful comparative map is:

```text
NYĀYA
│
├── Reliable speaker
├── Word
├── Sentence
└── Verbal cognition

MĪMĀṂSĀ
│
├── Vedic authority
├── Sentence
├── Dharma
└── Interpretation

VYĀKARAṆA / BHARTṚHARI
│
├── Śabda
├── Sphoṭa
├── Sentence
└── Cognition
```

These are not interchangeable theories.

---

# 9. Language and Cognition

Language becomes epistemological when it produces knowledge.

The key question is:

> How does a sequence of sounds or words produce cognition of something?

Study the full chain:

```text
Sound
 ↓
Word recognition
 ↓
Meaning
 ↓
Sentence relation
 ↓
Sentence cognition
 ↓
Knowledge / action
```

Different traditions locate the decisive explanatory factor at different points.

---

# 10. Language and Reality

The deeper philosophical question is:

> Is language merely a tool for describing reality, or does linguistic structure reveal something about cognition and reality itself?

Bhartṛhari makes this question especially important.

Nyāya, by contrast, develops a more differentiated account of words, meanings, objects, and verbal cognition.

---

# 11. Modern Comparisons

Possible modern areas:

- semantics;
- pragmatics;
- reference;
- compositionality;
- natural-language processing;
- programming languages;
- language models;
- speech recognition.

Use these as comparisons, not replacements for the classical theories.

For example:

```text
Classical question:
How does a sentence produce unified cognition?

Modern comparison:
How is sentence meaning represented computationally?
```

The two questions may illuminate one another without being identical.

## Core Principle

> **The philosophy of language begins where the simple assumption that words merely "name things" stops being sufficient.**
