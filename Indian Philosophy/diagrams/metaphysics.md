# Metaphysics

Metaphysics in this repository concerns questions about reality, causation, self, mind, universals, particulars, relations, action, time, space, dharma, and liberation.

The principal source base is Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika, with Mīmāṃsā and other materials providing important comparative perspectives.

---

# 1. Fundamental Questions

- What exists?
- What kinds of things exist?
- What distinguishes one entity from another?
- How are properties related to substances?
- What is causation?
- What is the self?
- What is mind?
- What is time?
- What is space?
- What is absence?
- What is liberation?

---

# 2. Ontology and Epistemology

A metaphysical claim should always be paired with an epistemological question.

```text
Claim about reality
       ↓
What is its category?
       ↓
How is it known?
       ↓
What argument establishes it?
       ↓
What objection challenges it?
```

This is especially important in Nyāya.

---

# 3. Substance

*Dravya* is a central ontological category.

The Vaiśeṣika material analyzes substances including:

- Earth
- Water
- Fire
- Air
- Ether
- Time
- Space
- Self
- Mind

The text also examines their attributes, causal roles, and modes of existence.

---

# 4. Qualities

*Guṇa* refers to qualities or attributes.

The ontology distinguishes qualities from substances and actions.

Examples discussed in the tradition include:

- colour;
- taste;
- smell;
- touch;
- number;
- magnitude;
- cognition;
- pleasure;
- pain;
- desire;
- aversion.

---

# 5. Action

*Karma* in the Vaiśeṣika ontological sense is action or motion.

It must be distinguished from the broader ethical and karmic uses of the same word.

This is a recurring example of why Sanskrit philosophical terms should be interpreted contextually.

---

# 6. Universal and Particular

### Sāmānya

Generality or universal.

### Viśeṣa

Particularity.

The Vaiśeṣika material treats these as important for explaining classification and individuality.

A philosophical problem arises:

> How can multiple distinct objects be genuinely of the same kind?

And conversely:

> What accounts for the ultimate distinctness of entities?

---

# 7. Samavāya

*Samavāya* is the relation of inherence.

It is invoked to explain relations such as:

- whole and parts;
- substance and qualities;
- universal and instances.

The repository's Nyāya material contains extended arguments concerning whether and how inherence can itself be cognized and what relation underlies the cognition of “inherence in this.”

This is an important entry point into advanced relational ontology.

---

# 8. Abhāva

*Abhāva* concerns absence or non-existence.

The philosophical problem is not merely:

> “Nothing is there.”

It is:

> What exactly is cognized when we cognize the absence of something?

Questions include:

- What is the locus of an absence?
- What is absent?
- What is the relation between absence and its counter-positive?
- How is absence known?

---

# 9. Time

Vaiśeṣika treats time as an ontological principle used to account for distinctions such as:

- now;
- then;
- simultaneous;
- earlier;
- later;
- slow;
- quick.

The text treats time as a substance and discusses its causal role.

---

# 10. Space

Space is treated as an ontological entity and as a basis for spatial relations.

The analysis distinguishes space from ordinary perceptible physical objects and examines its relation to qualities and causation.

---

# 11. Causation

Causation is one of the central metaphysical problems.

Ask:

```text
What is a cause?
What is an effect?
When does an effect arise?
What conditions are required?
Is every sequence causal?
```

The Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika tradition also analyzes different relations involved in causal production rather than reducing causation to simple temporal succession.

---

# 12. Self

The *ātman* is treated as distinct from:

- body;
- senses;
- mind.

Nyāya arguments seek to establish the self through cognition, desire, agency, memory, and other features.

The texts also examine whether the “I” refers primarily to the body or to the self.

---

# 13. Mind

*Manas* is treated as an internal organ in Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika.

It is not simply identical with the self.

The texts distinguish the cognizing subject from the instrument involved in mental cognition.

---

# 14. Dharma and Adharma

The Vaiśeṣika material treats *dharma* and *adharma* as important causal factors in the continuation of embodied existence.

Desire and aversion are connected with activity, while activity can generate dharma or adharma.

A broad causal sequence presented in the material is:

```text
Desire / Aversion
      ↓
Activity
      ↓
Dharma / Adharma
      ↓
Birth and death
      ↓
Continued experience
```

---

# 15. Liberation

The Vaiśeṣika commentary connects liberation with:

- knowledge of reality;
- removal of false cognition;
- cessation of faults;
- cessation of activity producing further bondage;
- cessation of the conditions supporting rebirth.

The text characterizes liberation as cessation of suffering and the continuation of the causal cycle of embodiment.

---

# 16. Metaphysical Method

For every metaphysical claim:

```text
1. Define the entity.
2. Identify its category.
3. Distinguish it from neighboring entities.
4. Identify its relations.
5. Explain how it is known.
6. Reconstruct the argument.
7. Examine counterarguments.
8. Test alternative explanations.
```

Potential comparison areas:

- ontology;
- metaphysics of properties;
- philosophy of mind;
- causation;
- identity;
- time;

- persistence.

Use modern philosophy as a comparative field, not as a replacement for the classical framework.

## Core Principle

> **Metaphysics asks what reality is like; Nyāya additionally demands an account of how we are justified in saying that reality is like that.**
