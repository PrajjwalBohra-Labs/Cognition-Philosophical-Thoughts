# Cognition

Cognition (*jñāna*, *buddhi*, *pratyaya* in different contexts) is one of the central problems connecting epistemology, logic, ontology, language, and philosophy of mind.

This document provides a structured reference for the study of cognition in the traditions represented in this repository.

---

## 1. Core Question

The central question is not simply:

> What is cognition?

It is also:

- What produces cognition?
- What is its object?
- Is it valid or erroneous?
- What conditions make it arise?
- How is cognition related to the self?
- How is one cognition related to another?
- How does cognition lead to action?
- How does language produce cognition?

---

## 2. Basic Distinctions

### Jñāna

A broad term for cognition or knowledge.

### Pramā

Valid cognition.

### Pramāṇa

A means or source of valid cognition.

### Saṃśaya

Doubt.

### Smṛti

Memory or recollection.

### Mithyājñāna

Erroneous cognition.

The Vaiśeṣika material distinguishes true knowledge from forms of false cognition such as doubt, error, dream, and uncertainty, while treating perception, inference, memory, and testimony among the forms of true cognition discussed there. [Source: *Vaiśeṣika Sūtras of Kaṇāda*, Book VIII–IX material.]

---

## 3. Production of Cognition

The Vaiśeṣika commentary explicitly analyzes the production of cognition through causal conditions.

One formulation associates cognition with the conjunction of:

```text
Self
  +
Mind
  +
Sense
  +
Object
      ↓
   Cognition
```

The text also distinguishes the roles of these factors rather than treating cognition as a simple product of the senses.

---

## 4. Cognition and Its Object

For every cognition ask:

```text
Cognition
│
├── Subject
├── Object
├── Mode
├── Cause
└── Epistemic status
```

Examples of questions:

- What is cognized?
- Is the object external or internal?
- Is the cognition perceptual or inferential?
- Is it presentative or recollective?
- Is it valid?
- What makes it valid?

---

## 5. Presentative and Representative Cognition

The Vaiśeṣika material distinguishes presentative cognition from representative cognition.

A useful study distinction is:

```text
Cognition
├── Presentative / anubhava
│      ├── Perception
│      └── Inference
│
└── Representative
       └── Memory / recollection
```

This scheme should be read as a textual classification rather than imposed universally on all Indian philosophical traditions.

---

## 6. Perceptual Cognition

Nyāya treats perception as distinct from inference.

The Nyāya discussions emphasize differences such as:

- perception's dependence upon the senses;
- inference's dependence upon a known inferential relation;
- inference's capacity to concern past, present, or future matters;
- the role of recollection in inference.

The texts explicitly reject reducing perception to inference. [Source: *Nyāya Sūtras*, Volumes I–II.]

---

## 7. Inferential Cognition

Inference involves a structured transition from a known reason to a conclusion.

```text
Perceived / known reason
        ↓
Established Vyāpti
        ↓
Application to Pakṣa
        ↓
Anumiti
```

Important terms:

- *Pakṣa* — subject
- *Sādhya* — property to be established
- *Hetu* — reason
- *Vyāpti* — invariable concomitance
- *Anumiti* — inferential cognition

---

## 8. Cognition Depending on Cognition

The Vaiśeṣika material discusses cases where one cognition depends upon another cognition.

Examples include expressions such as:

- “this”
- “that”
- “done by you”
- “feed him”

The analysis asks what prior cognition is required for the later cognition to arise.

This is important because cognition is not always treated as an isolated event.

---

## 9. Cognition and the Self

Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika connects cognition with the *ātman*.

The self is treated as the locus/support of cognition and related qualities, while *manas* functions as an internal organ involved in cognition.

The Nyāya texts also develop arguments intended to distinguish the self from:

- body;
- senses;
- mind;
- aggregates of these.

---

## 10. Cognition, Error and Suffering

A recurring philosophical structure is:

```text
False cognition
      ↓
Fault / attachment / aversion
      ↓
Action
      ↓
Consequences
      ↓
Suffering
```

The Vaiśeṣika commentary explicitly links false cognition concerning the self and non-self with the continuation of suffering and the need for knowledge of reality.

---

## 11. Cognition and Liberation

The texts connect knowledge of reality with the cessation of error and the conditions that sustain transmigration.

The Vaiśeṣika material presents knowledge of the real nature of the categories as part of the path toward the highest good.

Thus cognition is not merely theoretical:

```text
Correct cognition
      ↓
Removal of false cognition
      ↓
Reduction of faults
      ↓
Cessation of action producing further bondage
      ↓
Liberation
```

---

## 12. Language and Cognition

Verbal cognition creates another major problem:

> How does an utterance produce cognition of something not presently perceived?

Nyāya analyzes *śabda* through reliable testimony.

Mīmāṃsā gives a different account of verbal authority and interpretation.

Bhartṛhari's *Vākyapadīya* approaches language and cognition through a different philosophical framework centered on *śabda*, sentence, meaning, and *sphoṭa*.

---

## 13. Analytical Framework

For any cognition, record:

```text
1. What is cognized?
2. What is its object?
3. What produced it?
4. Is it presentative or recollective?
5. Is it perceptual, inferential, comparative, or verbal?
6. Is it valid?
7. What establishes its validity?
8. What rival explanation is possible?
9. What action follows from it?
10. What philosophical consequences follow?
```

## Core Principle

> Cognition should be studied simultaneously as an epistemic event, a causal event, a linguistic possibility, and a philosophical problem concerning the knower and the known.
