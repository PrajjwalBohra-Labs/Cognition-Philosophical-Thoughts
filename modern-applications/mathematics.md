# Mathematics and Formal Reasoning

Mathematics provides an especially clear environment for studying definition, inference, proof, contradiction, abstraction, and conceptual precision.

The purpose of this file is not to claim that Nyāya is a mathematical proof system. It is to use mathematics as a disciplined environment for developing rigorous reasoning.

---

# 1. Why Mathematics Matters

Mathematical reasoning requires:

- precise definitions;
- explicit assumptions;
- valid inference;
- consistency;
- counterexample testing;
- abstraction;
- proof.

These skills reinforce philosophical analysis.

---

# 2. Definition Before Argument

A mathematical proof depends upon definitions.

Use:

```text
Term
 ↓
Definition
 ↓
Properties
 ↓
Assumptions
 ↓
Theorem
 ↓
Proof
```

This parallels the philosophical discipline of clarifying terms before debating conclusions.

---

# 3. Proposition and Proof

A useful structure is:

```text
Claim
↓
Given assumptions
↓
Relevant definitions
↓
Lemmas
↓
Inference
↓
Conclusion
```

For each step ask:

> Does the conclusion actually follow?

---

# 4. Counterexamples

To disprove a universal statement:

```text
Claim:
For every X, P(X).

Search:
One X such that not-P(X).
```

This is an excellent training exercise for the analysis of *vyāpti*.

---

# 5. Necessary and Sufficient Conditions

Learn to distinguish:

```text
Necessary:
Without X, Y cannot occur.

Sufficient:
If X occurs, Y follows.
```

Then examine:

```text
X → Y
Y → X
```

Do not confuse one with the other.

This distinction is valuable in philosophical definitions and causal claims.

---

# 6. Proof by Contradiction

A simplified structure:

```text
Assume not-P
 ↓
Derive contradiction
 ↓
Reject not-P
 ↓
Accept P
```

This is structurally comparable to the use of reductio-style reasoning in philosophical argument.

The similarity should not be mistaken for identity between mathematical proof methods and *tarka*.

---

# 7. Formal Logic

Useful concepts include:

- proposition;
- implication;
- conjunction;
- disjunction;
- negation;
- quantification;
- validity;
- satisfiability;
- contradiction.

Use formal logic as a modern tool for making inferential structure explicit.

---

# 8. Classical Comparison

A useful exercise is to take:

```text
Classical inference
        ↕
Formal argument
```

Ask:

- What is the subject?
- What is established?
- What is the reason?
- What general relation is required?
- What assumptions are implicit?
- Does the formalization preserve the original argument?

---

# 9. Avoiding Category Errors

Do not assume:

```text
Nyāya inference = modern formal logic
```

They answer different historical and philosophical questions.

Formal logic can nevertheless be used to model parts of an argument.

---

# 10. Mathematical Practice Routine

For every theorem:

```text
1. Write the exact statement.
2. Define every technical term.
3. List assumptions.
4. Try small examples.
5. Search for counterexamples.
6. Identify the proof strategy.
7. Prove it.
8. Try to break the proof.
9. Rewrite the proof clearly.
```

---

# 11. Practical Projects

### Project A — Definition Testing

Choose a mathematical definition.

Find:

- clear positive cases;
- clear negative cases;
- borderline cases.

Explain why the definition includes or excludes each.

### Project B — Counterexample Notebook

Maintain:

```text
Claim
Counterexample
Why it works
What the claim should have said
```

### Project C — Proof Reconstruction

Take a proof and rewrite every step as:

```text
Known
+
Rule
=
New conclusion
```

---

# 12. Mathematics and Philosophy

Mathematics can train:

- precision;
- abstraction;
- consistency;
- formal inference;
- tolerance for technical detail.

But philosophical questions may require analysis of:

- meaning;
- ontology;
- epistemic justification;
- interpretation;
- value.

Formalization does not automatically resolve those questions.
