# Curriculum

A structured learning path for the systematic study of Indian philosophy, designed around the books and resources contained in this repository.

The curriculum progresses from foundational concepts to increasingly technical work in epistemology, logic, debate, language, cognition, ontology, metaphysics, Mīmāṃsā, and Navya-Nyāya.

It is designed for serious self-study. The aim is not merely to acquire information about philosophical systems, but to develop the ability to **understand, reconstruct, compare, criticize, and apply philosophical arguments**.

---

## Guiding Principles

### 1. Primary textual grounding

The books in `books/` form the textual foundation of the curriculum.

The principal areas represented in the present collection are:

- Nyāya
- Navya-Nyāya
- Vaiśeṣika
- Mīmāṃsā
- Vyākaraṇa
- Philosophy of language
- Epistemology
- Logic and inference
- Ontology and metaphysics

### 2. Progressive difficulty

The sequence begins with accessible conceptual foundations and gradually introduces increasingly technical terminology and argumentation.

### 3. Cross-traditional study

Indian philosophical traditions should not be studied as isolated systems. Important questions are repeatedly examined from different perspectives.

### 4. Argument before conclusion

A doctrine should be studied through the problem it addresses, the argument offered for it, the objections raised against it, and the response given.

### 5. Classical precision with modern relevance

Modern examples may be used to clarify difficult concepts, but classical concepts should not be silently equated with modern theories.

# Study Method

For each lesson, use the following cycle:

```text
READ
  ↓
UNDERSTAND
  ↓
RECONSTRUCT
  ↓
QUESTION
  ↓
COMPARE
  ↓
CRITICIZE
  ↓
APPLY
  ↓
REVIEW
```

### Read

Study the assigned section of the relevant book.

### Understand

Identify the terminology and basic philosophical position.

### Reconstruct

Reconstruct the argument in your own words.

### Question

Ask what assumptions the argument depends upon.

### Compare

Examine how another school approaches the same problem.

### Criticize

Look for counterexamples, ambiguities, hidden assumptions, and possible objections.

### Apply

Test the reasoning against an appropriate modern or everyday example.

### Review

Return to the text and determine whether the modern example actually illuminates the classical position or distorts it.

# Master Learning Objectives

By completing the curriculum, the learner should be able to:

- Explain the major concepts of Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika.
- Understand the principal pramāṇas.
- Analyze perception and inference.
- Construct and evaluate Nyāya arguments.
- Identify defective reasons and argumentative errors.
- Understand classical debate structures.
- Analyze competing theories of language and meaning.
- Understand major Mīmāṃsā concerns regarding authority and interpretation.
- Explain important Vaiśeṣika ontological categories.
- Analyze Nyāya accounts of self, mind, cognition, causation, and liberation.
- Read technical Navya-Nyāya discussions with increasing independence.
- Compare philosophical positions without conflating them.
- Apply rigorous argument analysis to contemporary problems.
- Formulate and defend an original philosophical position.

---

# Completion Standard

A level should not be considered complete merely because the assigned pages have been read.

A level is complete when the learner can:

1. Explain its central concepts without relying on memorized definitions.
2. Use its technical terminology correctly.
3. Reconstruct its major arguments.
4. Explain important objections.
5. Compare relevant rival positions.
6. Identify unresolved philosophical problems.
7. Apply the analytical method independently.

The ultimate objective is **philosophical competence**, not completion of a reading list.
