# Debate

Debate is treated in the Nyāya tradition not merely as verbal conflict but as a structured philosophical activity involving positions, reasons, objections, replies, and recognized defects.

This document provides a working framework for studying debate and argumentation.

---

## 1. Central Problem

A philosophical debate asks:

> How can disagreement be transformed into rational examination?

This requires distinguishing:

- thesis from assertion;
- reason from rhetoric;
- objection from contradiction;
- refutation from mere denial;
- genuine inquiry from victory-seeking.

## 2. Core Vocabulary

| Term | Working meaning |
|---|---|
| **Vāda** | Philosophical discussion oriented toward establishing what is correct |
| **Jalpa** | Adversarial disputation |
| **Vitaṇḍā** | Destructive criticism without establishing an independent thesis |
| **Tarka** | Reasoning used to expose consequences of a supposition |
| **Nirṇaya** | Determination or settled judgment |
| **Hetvābhāsa** | Fallacious or defective reason |
| **Chala** | Equivocation or misconstruction |
| **Jāti** | Futile or adverse rejoinder |
| **Nigrahasthāna** | Ground of defeat in debate |

The Vaiśeṣika commentary also explicitly enumerates *pramāṇa, prameya, saṃśaya, prayojana, dṛṣṭānta, siddhānta, avayava, tarka, nirṇaya, vāda, jalpa, vitaṇḍā, hetvābhāsa, chala, jāti,* and *nigrahasthāna* among the analytical categories discussed in the wider system.

---

## 3. Vāda

The ideal of *vāda* is closest to philosophical inquiry.

The basic structure is:

```text
Question
   ↓
Position
   ↓
Reasons
   ↓
Objection
   ↓
Reply
   ↓
Determination
```

The objective is not simply to defeat a person but to establish or clarify the correct position.

---

## 4. Jalpa

*Jalpa* is more adversarial.

It involves the attempt to establish one's own position and demolish the opponent's position.

The important distinction is between:

```text
Seeking truth
      vs.
Seeking victory
```

The two may overlap, but they should not be confused.

---

## 5. Vitaṇḍā

*Vitaṇḍā* focuses on criticism of the opponent without necessarily establishing an independent thesis.

This produces an important methodological question:

> Is successfully criticizing an opponent sufficient to establish one's own position?

The Nyāya framework treats this distinction as philosophically significant.

---

## 6. Tarka

*Tarka* is reasoning used to expose the unacceptable consequences of an assumed position.

A useful structure is:

```text
Assume X
   ↓
X entails consequence Y
   ↓
Y is unacceptable / incompatible with established knowledge
   ↓
Therefore reconsider X
```

*Tarka* is not simply identical with an independent *pramāṇa*.

---

## 7. Nirṇaya

*Nirṇaya* is determination following examination.

The movement can be represented as:

```text
Doubt
 ↓
Examination
 ↓
Reasons
 ↓
Objections
 ↓
Tarka
 ↓
Determination
```

## 9. Grounds of Defeat

The Nyāya material treats debate as governed by procedural and argumentative standards.

Examples include:

- failure to understand the opponent;
- failure to answer;
- failure to maintain one's thesis;
- contradiction;
- inappropriate response;
- failure to reproduce the relevant opponent's argument before answering it.

The texts show that debate defects are not merely rhetorical faults; they can concern the logical structure and procedural integrity of the exchange.

---

## 10. Argument Reconstruction

Before criticizing an argument, reconstruct it fairly.

```text
1. What is the thesis?
2. What is the reason?
3. What is the intended relation?
4. What example supports it?
5. What objection is being answered?
6. What exactly does the reply establish?
7. What does it fail to establish?
```

This should be the standard method throughout the repository.

## 12. Modern Applications

The same analytical discipline can be applied to:

- academic disagreement;
- legal argument;
- scientific disputes;
- public policy;
- technical design reviews;
- software architecture discussions;
- online arguments.

The goal is not to force modern discourse into classical categories. The goal is to use the classical discipline of reconstructing positions, reasons, objections, and replies.

---

## 13. Practical Rules

Before responding to an argument:

1. State the opponent's position accurately.
2. Identify the strongest version of the argument.
3. Separate premises from conclusions.
4. Identify the intended inferential relation.
5. Test for counterexamples.
6. Distinguish refutation from disagreement.
7. State what your criticism actually establishes.
