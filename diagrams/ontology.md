# Ontology

Ontology is the systematic study of what exists and of the categories, properties, relations, and modes of being through which reality is analyzed.

In this repository, the principal ontological framework is Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika.

---

# 1. The Padārtha Framework

The core structure is:

```text
Padārtha
│
├── Dravya
├── Guṇa
├── Karma
├── Sāmānya
├── Viśeṣa
├── Samavāya
└── Abhāva
```

The exact historical treatment of these categories should always be checked against the particular text and period.

---

# 2. Dravya — Substance

*Dravya* is a fundamental ontological category.

The Vaiśeṣika material enumerates nine substances:

```text
1. Earth
2. Water
3. Fire
4. Air
5. Ether
6. Time
7. Space
8. Self
9. Mind
```

The text proceeds by examining their attributes, causal roles, and distinctive characteristics.

---

# 3. Guṇa — Quality

*Guṇa* refers to qualities that exist in substances.

The system includes a broad range of qualities, including perceptible qualities and mental qualities.

Examples include:

- colour;
- taste;
- smell;
- touch;
- number;
- magnitude;
- cognition;
- pleasure;
- pain;
- desire;
- aversion.

A key ontological question is:

> Can a quality exist independently, or must it exist in a substance?

---

# 4. Karma — Action

In the ontological classification, *karma* means action or motion.

It is distinct from:

- *guṇa* — quality;
- ethical *karma* — action and its consequences.

This distinction should always be maintained when reading across traditions.

---

# 5. Sāmānya — Universal

*Sāmānya* explains commonality across instances.

For example, the fact that multiple entities are recognized as belonging to a common kind requires an account of generality.

The philosophical problem is:

```text
Many individuals
      ↓
Common character
      ↓
Recognition of a class
```

The Vaiśeṣika tradition treats universals as ontologically significant rather than merely linguistic conveniences.

---

# 6. Viśeṣa — Particularity

*Viśeṣa* accounts for ultimate particularity or distinction.

The repository's Vaiśeṣika material emphasizes that particularity prevents all entities of a relevant kind from collapsing into indistinguishability.

The contrast is:

```text
Sāmānya → commonality
Viśeṣa  → particularity
```

---

# 7. Samavāya — Inherence

*Samavāya* is a distinctive relation used to explain inseparable connections.

Examples include:

```text
Whole ↔ Parts
Quality ↔ Substance
Universal ↔ Instance
```

The philosophical challenge is to explain a relation that is not simply ordinary conjunction.

The Nyāya texts contain detailed debates about how inherence is related to cognition and what sort of relation can ground the cognition of an entity “in” another.

---

# 8. Abhāva — Absence

Absence introduces negation into ontology.

Study:

- what is absent;
- where it is absent;
- what its counter-positive is;
- how absence is cognized.

A useful representation is:

```text
Locus
  +
Absence
  +
Counter-positive
      ↓
Cognition of absence
```

---

# 9. Categories and Cognition

Ontology is connected to epistemology because categories must be cognized.

The Vaiśeṣika material analyzes how substances, qualities, actions, universals, and other entities become objects of cognition.

Thus:

```text
Ontology
   ↕
Epistemology
   ↕
Cognition
```

---

# 10. Ontology and Error

The Vaiśeṣika commentary explicitly connects doubt and error with failure to apprehend distinguishing features.

A useful pattern is:

```text
Common feature perceived
        +
Distinctive feature not apprehended
        ↓
Doubt / error
        ↓
Distinctive knowledge
        ↓
Correct cognition
```

This is philosophically important because ontology and epistemology interact directly.

---

# 11. Ontology of Self

The self is treated as a substance.

The Nyāya arguments distinguish it from:

- body;
- sense organs;
- mind;
- aggregates.

The Vaiśeṣika material treats cognition and related qualities as relevant to the analysis of the self.

---

# 12. Ontology of Mind

*Manas* is a distinct internal organ/substance in the system.

It is not simply the self and should not be equated with the modern concept of brain.

Its philosophical role must be understood through the causal account of cognition.

---

# 13. Ontology of Time and Space

Time and space are included among substances.

They are used to account for:

- temporal ordering;
- simultaneity;
- distance;
- location;
- spatial distinction.

The text treats time as an efficient causal factor in relevant contexts.

---

# 14. Ontology and Liberation

Ontology is not purely descriptive.

The system connects correct knowledge of reality with liberation.

A broad structure is:

```text
Correct knowledge of categories
        ↓
Removal of false cognition
        ↓
Reduction of faults
        ↓
Cessation of bondage-producing activity
        ↓
Liberation
```

---

# 15. Ontological Analysis Template

For every entity:

```text
Name:
Sanskrit:

Category:
What is it?

Locus:
Where does it exist?

Qualities:
What properties does it possess?

Relations:
What is it related to?

Cause:
How does it arise?

Epistemic access:
How is it known?

Distinctive feature:
What separates it from similar entities?

Philosophical function:
Why is it needed?
```

---

# 16. Comparative Questions

For each category ask:

- Is it independently real?
- Is it reducible to another category?
- Is it merely conceptual?
- How is it known?
- What alternative ontology could explain the same phenomena?
- What argument establishes it?

## Core Principle

> **Ontology is not merely a catalogue of things; it is an argument about what kinds of entities and relations are required to make sense of experience and reasoning.**
